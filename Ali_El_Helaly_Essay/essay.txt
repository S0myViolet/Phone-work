The first time someone asked me what I liked besides football, I didn't have an answer.

I had spent years training at academy level, most recently on a scholarship at a LaLiga academy that took me to tournaments abroad. Football wasn't a hobby to me; I assumed it was my future. Then a routine checkup revealed I needed surgery on both legs and would be off the field for eight months. My friendships, my schedule, and the way I saw myself were all built around the game. Without it, I wasn't sure what was left.

At first, I stayed close to the game, standing on the sidelines while my teammates talked about plays I wasn't part of. It only made me feel further away. So I stopped holding on to who I had been and started paying attention to what else was in front of me.

It began with school. For years I had done just enough to keep up, because my real effort went into training. With that time suddenly free, I gave it to my classes, and economics caught my attention. Once I started, I noticed it everywhere, from the price of new boots to the headlines my parents discussed at dinner. That curiosity led me to AP Microeconomics, where I learned the models behind what I had been noticing. For the first time, understanding something difficult gave me the same satisfaction as a good game.

Those dinners were new too. Training had taken most of my evenings and weekends for years, and I hadn't realized how little I was home. That time with my family reminded me that the people who mattered most had nothing to do with whether I could play.

When I was finally cleared to return, my legs weren't ready. The surgery had healed, but my body hadn't fully recovered, and I wasn't the player I had been. A year earlier, that would have crushed me. This time, it didn't. Football still mattered, but it was no longer the only thing holding me up. The physical setback was real. The mental one never came.

Those eight months taught me that obstacles feel smaller when your whole identity isn't riding on one thing. Coming back to a body that wasn't ready was the first time I tested that, and it's how I approach setbacks now. I ask what else they make room for, and I make sure no single thing holds me up.

I'll bring that mindset to IE, where I want to study business administration and turn my curiosity about how markets work into something practical. Moving to a new country, working at a harder academic level, and building a life away from my family will all test me, and not every day will go well. But I've already rebuilt my sense of who I am once, and I know I can do it again.
