The first time someone asked me what I liked besides football, I didn't have an answer.

I had spent years training at academy level, most recently on a scholarship at a LaLiga academy that took me to tournaments abroad. Football wasn't a hobby to me; I assumed it was my future. Then a routine checkup ended with my doctor telling me I needed surgery on both legs and would be off the field for eight months. My friendships, my schedule, and the way I saw myself had all been built around the game. Without it, I wasn't sure what was left.

At first, I tried to stay close to the game. I stood on the sidelines and listened to my teammates talk about plays I wasn't part of. It only made me feel further away. So I stopped holding on to who I had been and started paying attention to what else was in front of me.

It began with school. For years I had done just enough to keep up, because my real effort went into training. With that time suddenly free, I gave it to my classes instead, and economics caught my attention. I had never thought about why prices rise and fall, or how millions of individual decisions add up to a market. Once I started, I noticed it everywhere, from the price of a new pair of boots to the headlines my parents discussed at dinner. That curiosity led me to AP Microeconomics, where I learned the models behind what I had been noticing, from supply and demand to how firms decide what to charge. For the first time, understanding something difficult gave me the same satisfaction as a good game.

Those dinners were new too. Practices and games had taken most of my evenings and weekends for years, and I hadn't realized how little I was actually home. Spending that time with my family reminded me that the people who mattered most to me had nothing to do with whether I could play.

When I was finally cleared to return, my legs weren't ready. The surgery had healed, but my body hadn't fully recovered, and I wasn't the player I had been. A year earlier, that would have crushed me. This time, it didn't. Football still mattered, but it was no longer the only thing holding me up. I had my studies, my family, and a clearer sense of who I was off the field. The physical setback was real. The mental one never came.

Those eight months taught me that obstacles feel smaller when your whole identity isn't riding on one thing. I used to see setbacks as something to push through until life went back to normal. Now, when something closes a door, I ask what it makes room for.

For me, that room was economics, and it's what I want to study at IE. I want to learn it somewhere that connects theory to how markets actually behave, alongside classmates from all over the world. Moving to a new country and starting over won't be easy. But I've already rebuilt my sense of who I am once, and I know I can do it again.
